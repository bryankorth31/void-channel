Mind · a course we wrote

Critical thinking

Most of what reaches you every day is somebody's claim: an ad, a post, a doctor's pamphlet, a boss, a news story, a relative at dinner. Nobody can check everything, so the skill is knowing which claims to trust, which to test, and how. People who never learn it spend their money, their votes and their health on other people's say-so.

High schoolYoung adultsEveryday life

By the end you will be able to...
  1. Tell a claim from its evidence.
  2. Spot the standard ways an argument cheats.
  3. Know what a number can and cannot say.
  4. Notice when a story is doing the thinking for you.
  5. Change your mind in public without shame.
  6. Read a hard text without an intermediary telling you what it means.

You will be able to: Tell a claim from its evidence.

Why it matters

Before you can weigh anything, you have to see that "this works" and "here is how we know" are two different things. Most people hear the first and assume the second.

How this reaches you

Supplements, miracle cleaners, "studies show", the phone call that says your car warranty is expiring. Each one is a claim asking for your money before it shows its evidence.

You will be able to: Notice when a story is doing the thinking for you.

Why it matters

A good story feels true because it is well told, not because it is true. Whole countries have bet everything on a story everybody around them believed.

How this reaches you

Every bubble works like this: tulips in 1637, houses in 2007, whatever is next. So do feeds that keep you angry. If everyone around you is sure, that is the moment to check.

Listen

Charles Mackay, Extraordinary Popular Delusions (1841), read for LibriVox · 21 min

Who teaches this unit

  • Neil Postman, teacher and media critic. Forty years in the classroom; saw what television would do to public argument.
  • Charles Mackay, journalist. Chronicled the bubbles and manias of three centuries in 1841.

You will be able to: Change your mind in public without shame.

Why it matters

Being wrong is normal. Staying wrong to save face is what does the damage. The people we most admire for thinking clearly are the ones who said "I was wrong" out loud.

How this reaches you

At work, at home, in a family argument that has run for years. The person who can say "I hadn't thought of that" ends up trusted.

Listen
On Liberty, chapter 2 (first half): Of the Liberty of Thought and Discussion
John Stuart Mill (1859), read for LibriVox · 53 min

Why even a wrong opinion should be heard: the person who only knows their own side of a case knows little even of that.

Public domain (LibriVox). Source

Do this week

Who teaches this unit

  • Jacob Bronowski, mathematician and historian of science. A working scientist who made The Ascent of Man.
  • John Stuart Mill, philosopher and economist. Wrote the case for free thought and discussion that is still quoted in courts.

You will be able to: Read a hard text without an intermediary telling you what it means.

Why it matters

Knowing the name of a thing is not knowing the thing. Every summary is someone's choice of what to leave out, so the habit worth having is going to the source.

How this reaches you

Your lease, your insurance policy, a law people are fighting about, a study a headline is quoting. The original is usually shorter and plainer than people assume.
