Your money, plainly

Everyone earns, spends, saves and borrows, and most people are never taught how risk, interest and markets really work. Yale's Robert Shiller taught this course in 2008, in the middle of the crash; we add everyday pictures and quizzes.

From Financial Markets (ECON 252, 2008), Robert J. Shiller, Yale University, spring 2008. CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 US.

High schoolYoung adultsEveryday life

By the end you will be able to...
  1. See finance as a tool for sharing risk, like an engine: strong, useful, and dangerous to people who do not understand it.
  2. Read an interest rate as the price of time, and take inflation off it to see what you really gain or pay.
  3. Judge a loan by the payment in its hardest year, not its easiest, and know how home loans got safer and then riskier again.
  4. Explain why insurance works only with many people in the pool, and spot moral hazard, selection bias and the "I can't afford it" trap.

  6. Name the four mind traps Shiller shows in money choices: overconfidence, loss aversion, the pull of certainty, and regret, and spot them in yourself.
  7. Explain how a bank lends out most of what it holds, why a rumor can start a run, and what keeps your deposits safe.
  8. Spot the weak spots sellers use, read the "label" every public investment must carry, and know what is insured and what is not.

5 questions, each answer quotes the lecture

1. Who does Shiller say finance touches?

Even a poet ends up talking money with a publisher. Rent, pay, loans and insurance reach everyone.

"Just about any human endeavor involves finance."

2. When a risk is spread over many people, what happens to it?

The fire still costs what it costs. Sharing makes the hit on any one family small enough to survive.

"The idea is that if you spread risks they don't disappear, they're still there, but they're spread out over many people and the impact on any one person is reduced."

3. What does Shiller compare a financial instrument to?

Like an engine, it has many parts that must work together, and new designs often fail before people learn to build them right.

"A financial instrument is like an engineering device."

4. What is a "subprime" borrower?

In 2008 these loans were failing in record numbers, and the crash spread from them through the whole system.

"is somebody who has a poor credit history or some other indication that would suggest that they might not be able to repay the mortgage"

5. What danger comes with bringing more people into finance?

Shiller wants finance open to everyone, and warns that the people new to it are the easiest to take advantage of. That is why this course exists.

"less capable and more vulnerable to exploitation"

Check yourself

5 questions, each answer quotes the lecture

1. What does Shiller say the line of interest rates (from short loans to long ones) shows?

An interest rate is what it costs to have money now instead of later. Longer waits can cost more or less, and that line shows it.

"it shows the price of time at various maturities"

2. How does a Treasury bill pay you?

You pay about $99.58 today and get $100 back later. The gap is your interest.

"a discount bond does not pay interest; it's sold at a discount."

3. Old bonds framed on a wall still have every coupon attached. What does that mean?

Each coupon was cut off and cashed when a payment came due. Coupons still attached mean the payments stopped.

"It means the company went bankrupt and never paid; otherwise, the coupons would have been clipped."

4. Interest is about 3% and prices are rising about 3%. What is the real interest rate?

Real interest is the rate minus inflation. Your money grows, but so do prices, so it buys the same as before.

"Right now, we have a Federal Funds Rate of about 3% and an inflation rate of around 3%, so right now the real interest rate is close to 0."

5. Who sets the market's interest rates?

Rates come out of millions of people borrowing and lending. Dealers and even the government's auctions have to follow the market.

"nobody really knows where these interest rates come from because no one person sets them."

Check yourself

6 questions, each answer quotes the lecture

1. What is a mortgage?

If you stop paying, the lender can take the property. That is why lenders will lend for a home at all.

"Mortgages are debts secured by property as collateral."

2. In the 1920s, a mortgage lasted about five years, and then the whole loan came due. What happened after 1929?

Jobs and home values fell, banks would not refinance, and the big final payment could not be met.

"They would lose the house. That happened in huge numbers in the 1930s."

3. What changed when mortgages became "self-amortizing" in 1933?

Paying a little of the loan every month means no giant payment waiting at the end.

"So, when the mortgage ends, you're just clear and free; you don't have to pay anything"

4. What is a "two and twenty-eight" loan?

Many people who took these did not understand that the payment would jump after two years, and some could not afford the new one.

"A typical ARM is two and twenty-eight, which means that it has a low teaser rate for two years"

5. Early in a 30-year mortgage, where does most of each payment go?

The balance falls slowly at first. Selling after a few years can leave you owing almost what you borrowed.

"When you first buy a house most of your payment is going to interest."

6. In 1988, how many Los Angeles home buyers worried they would be priced out forever unless they bought now?

Fear of missing out pushed prices up. In steady Milwaukee only 27% felt it.

"We had 80% agreement with that in Los Angeles; so people were really worried."

Check yourself

6 questions, each answer quotes the lecture

1. What does risk pooling mean?

Any one person's bad luck is spread over everyone in the pool.

"Risk pooling means they put a lot of people with independent or low-correlated risk into a pool and reduce the risk for the whole pool."

2. Why does an insurer need a lot of customers?

With 100 policies the payouts could swing a lot; with 10,000 they barely move.

"You have to be a big company to do it"

3. What is the classic "moral hazard"?

Being insured can change what people do. Policies are written to guard against it.

"The classic moral hazard problem is the problem that you give fire insurance on a house and someone burns down the house in order to collect the insurance."

4. What is "selection bias" for an insurer?

Sick people want life insurance most. That is why insurers ask questions before they sell.

"if you offer insurance policies you will tend to attract people who are higher risk."

5. Poor farmers in Africa told an insurer "I can't afford crop insurance." What does Shiller say insurance is really for?

The good year is exactly when you can pay for the bad one.

"The whole idea of insurance is that you take from your good years and you move it into your bad years so that you make it through all your years."

6. What is "term" life insurance?

Term is the simplest, and usually the cheapest, way to protect a family while children are young.

"This is insurance that you pay each year for insurance in that year and it does not build a cash value."

Check yourself

6 questions, each answer quotes the lecture

1. You own 1,000 shares of a company. How much of the company do you own?

If there are 1,000 shares in all, you own the company. If there are ten million, you own one ten-thousandth.

"It doesn't mean anything until you know how many shares are outstanding"

2. Your company does a two-for-one split and you now have twice as many shares. How much richer are you?

Every shareholder's shares doubled, and so did the total. Your slice is the same size.

"Don't be too jubilant because when they do a split they do it to every single shareholder."

3. What is dilution?

New shares given to a CEO, for example, make every other holder's slice a little smaller.

"Dilution occurs when the company changes the number of shares asymmetrically"

4. Over the long run, about how many years of earnings have people usually paid for a share?

When the ratio shot far above that, to 35 in 1929 and about 46 in 2000, prices later fell hard.

"So, typically the ratio of price-to-earnings has been about fifteen. That means that people will pay up front for about fifteen years' earnings."

5. You buy a $300,000 house with $30,000 down, and prices fall 10%. What happened to your $30,000?

Borrowing magnifies both gains and losses. A small fall can wipe out a small down payment.

"Your $30,000 down payment is completely wiped out because you were leveraged."

6. A broker says "buy today, before the stock goes ex-dividend, or you'll miss the dividend!" Is that good advice?

Brokers are told not to "sell dividends" like this, because it misleads buyers.

"That's misleading because it really doesn't matter when you buy the stock"

Check yourself

6 questions, each answer quotes the lecture

1. Yale students gave ranges they were 90% sure held the right answer. How often were they right about the Statue of Liberty's weight?

People are sure far more often than they are right, and smart people are no exception.

"It should have been 90% who were right."

2. Irving Fisher, Yale's star economist, called the 1929 stock market a "permanently high plateau." What happened to him?

He had borrowed against his home. Yale bought the house and rented it back to him.

"He had a beautiful mansion; he was a wealthy man for a while, but he lost everything in the stock market crash."

3. Why do people turn down a coin flip that pays $200 on heads and costs $100 on tails?

Kahneman and Tversky called it the kink in how we value money: right where we stand, losses weigh more.

"What it means is that I value losses much more than I value gains from wherever I am."

4. Most people take $3,000 for sure over an 80% chance at $4,000, yet pick the bigger prize when both chances are small. Why?

In the mind there are three chances: can't happen, might happen, certain. Certain gets far more weight than it should.

"I think people like certainty and ambiguity is difficult for them to adjust to."

5. Why do many investors sell a stock right after it goes up?

Fear of regret, not a calculation, drives the sale.

"when the stock market goes up they try to sell it and lock in the gain because they are worried that if it goes down again they will regret not having sold it"

6. What are "mental compartments"?

Casino winnings or a refund can feel like play money, even though a dollar is a dollar.

"People put things in mental compartments, all different compartments in your mind, and you have separate values for things depending on which compartment they're in."

Check yourself

6 questions, each answer quotes the lecture

1. In Shiller's story of how banking began, what did the goldsmith do?

Customers did not care which coins came back, so the goldsmith kept a fraction on hand and lent the rest.

"Why I don't lend some of it out and I can earn interest on the loan and make money."

2. How does a bank make its money?

The gap between the two rates is the bank's profit.

"it charges a higher interest on the loan than they pay on the deposit. The difference is their profit; that's how they make money."

3. What does it mean that banks "lend long and borrow short"?

That is the useful part, and also the weak spot: if every depositor asks at once, the money is out on long loans.

"Liquidity is that banks lend long and borrow short"

4. Why are banks regulated, according to Shiller?

Runs have happened so often in history that governments now require banks to hold reserves and capital.

"if one bank gets in trouble and can't pay out on its deposits, then that can bring the whole system down because it can cause a panic among investors"

5. Northern Rock's depositors were insured, yet they lined up to pull their money out in 2007. Why?

Details of deposit insurance matter. Britain fixed the limit after the run.

"yes but only up to £3,000"

6. What changed at the big rating agencies after John Moody died?

Like a teacher paid by the students for grades, it invites bias. They gave AAA ratings to some subprime securities.

"they started accepting fees from the people they rate; some people think that you shouldn't do that."

You will be able to: Explain how a bank lends out most of what it holds, why a rumor can start a run, and what keeps your deposits safe.

Why it matters

Banking began when goldsmiths lent out gold that customers had left in their safes, keeping only a fraction on hand. That works on normal days. When a rumor makes everyone ask at once, no bank has it all. In 2007 savers lined up outside Britain's Northern Rock, the first British bank run since 1866.

How this reaches you

Your checking and savings accounts, a credit union, deposit insurance, money apps that hold your cash but are not banks, the ratings on investments.

Check yourself

6 questions, each answer quotes the lecture

1. Why does finance need rules, according to Shiller?

Wishful thinking, anchoring and gambling urges are well known, and some sellers use them on purpose.

"People who know human psychology know that they can exploit human weaknesses."

2. What did the realtors' ads say in 2008, just as prices were falling?

Written as a fact, by the people who earn from selling homes. That is overselling.

"home prices double every ten years"

3. A broker calls every week with a new trade idea. What is the danger?

It is called churning: the broker is paid per trade, so the calls help the broker, not you.

"if you keep trading too much, then you cannot possibly make money. The trading commissions will eat up all of your profits"

4. Louis Brandeis compared investment disclosure to what?

Just as food must list what is inside, a public investment must show its facts to everyone equally.

"Sunshine is the best disinfectant."

5. Twenty-five people bought shares on a tip that started with a secretary at IBM. What happened to them?

Exchanges watch for unusual trading before big news, and trace it back.

"Those people are all in trouble because it's illegal to trade on insider information and they tracked them all down."

6. Is there an agency that pays you back if a stock falls or a broker lies to you?

Deposit insurance protects bank deposits, and brokerage insurance only replaces shares if the broker itself fails. Neither covers losses or bad advice.

"Only 16% of investors knew that there is no such company."

You will be able to: Spot the weak spots sellers use, read the "label" every public investment must carry, and know what is insured and what is not.

Why it matters

Shiller says the history of finance is a history of people being exploited, because sellers know the mind's weak spots: wishful thinking, anchoring on a number, gambling, and the illusion of control. The rules that make markets work came after scandals: disclosure ("sunshine is the best disinfectant"), the SEC in 1934, and surveillance that catches insider trading.

How this reaches you

Gold coin and "can't lose" ads, a broker or app that wants you to trade often, a tip from a friend of a friend, fees inside funds, "guaranteed" returns, deposit and brokerage insurance.
